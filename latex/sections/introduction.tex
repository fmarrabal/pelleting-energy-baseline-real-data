\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Improving energy performance in manufacturing means comparing the electricity an operation actually used with what it should have used under comparable conditions \citep{park2021}. Food manufacturing is a case in point. Its electricity goes to drives, refrigeration, pumping, mixing, cooling, conveying and process control; food processing in the United States has been estimated to consume about 75~TWh of electricity, and electricity accounts for roughly 30\% of the reported energy use of UK food and beverage manufacturing \citep{compton2018,foster2023}. Figures of that size only become actionable when consumption is read against production activity and operating conditions rather than in absolute terms. Energy management standards make the point explicit: ISO 50001 asks organizations to judge energy performance against a baseline normalized for the relevant variables, and ISO 50006 and the IPMVP describe how such baselines and performance indicators are built and used \citep{iso50001,iso50006,ipmvp2022}.

Compound-feed manufacturing is a representative setting because its production stages depend on electromechanical equipment whose demand varies with volume and process conditions. S\'anchez et al.\ \citep{sanchez2020} assessed motor efficiency and established energy baselines and performance indicators for a balanced-feed plant, reporting about 10~kWh/t at 500~t/day and an estimated saving potential of 364,000~kWh/year. Their work shows the value of normalizing consumption by production level, but the baseline was formulated at an aggregated process level; it did not deal with heterogeneous operation-level records in which product, pelleting line, batch organization and other descriptors change at the same time.

From a process perspective, pelleting is intrinsically multivariable. Pellet quality, conditioning, feed composition, steam application, temperature, particle characteristics and operating conditions all influence pelleting behaviour and mechanical energy demand \citep{thomas1996,thomas1997,thomas2020,skoch1981,bastiaansen2023}. These studies establish the physical basis for energy differences between operations, but their aims lie in material properties, process behaviour or pellet quality rather than in building and chronologically testing an operation-level electricity baseline from real plant records.

Specific energy consumption (SEC), usually in kWh/t, is simple and interpretable, but a single value cannot separate the effects of product mix, pelleting-line assignment, processed mass, batch structure and operating regime. Regression-based and data-driven baselines have therefore been used to estimate expected consumption under comparable conditions and to support measurement and verification \citep{kissock2008,granderson2016,gallagher2018,cosgrove2018,sanchez2020}. They demonstrate why expected energy use should be conditioned on production variables, but they have not been tested on heterogeneous feed-pelleting operations under a strict chronological protocol.

Data-driven modelling offers several ways to extend such baselines. Regularized linear regression gives stable, interpretable estimates when predictors are correlated \citep{hoerl1970}, and tree ensembles and neural networks can capture nonlinearities and interactions \citep{friedman2001}. Closest to our setting, Ekwaro-Osire et al.\ \citep{ekwaro2025} compared linear regression, tree-based methods and a multilayer perceptron on about 1,100 production batches with 67 input characteristics from an industrial compound-feed pelleting process and found that more complex models did not necessarily reduce error, which supports transparent statistical models as strong references. That study, however, relied on shuffled cross-validation and did not consider chronologically available histories of completed operations, pretrained temporal models, predictive uncertainty or record-level traceability within one comparison.

Temporal and pretrained time-series models add a further perspective. The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) combines recurrent layers, variable selection, gating and attention \citep{lim2021}; N-HiTS represents temporal structure through hierarchical interpolation \citep{challu2023}; and foundation models such as TimesFM and Chronos transfer representations learned from large time-series collections to new tasks \citep{das2024,ansari2024}. Industrial pelleting records differ from regularly sampled series, though: consecutive completed operations may have different masses, products, lines, start times and durations. The relevant question is whether a history restricted to operations completed before a target carries information beyond the descriptors of the target itself.

That question is inseparable from validation design. Random or shuffled partitions mix operating regimes across time and inflate apparent performance \citep{hewamalage2023}, so chronological evaluation is required whenever assessment should reflect the temporal direction of industrial use \citep{tashman2000,cerqueira2020,bergmeir2018}. The requirement tightens when historical outcomes become inputs, because only operations whose results were available before the target origin may enter its context. Development, calibration and testing must therefore stay temporally separated, and the value of history must be judged against a current-input model on identical eligible records.

Interpretability and uncertainty matter as well. Explainable-AI research has shown the value of local and global explanations alongside limitations in robustness, standardization and generalization \citep{saarela2024,cacao2025}. An industrial baseline should let every estimate be traced to the recorded production variables without presenting model terms as causal effects. Prediction intervals are needed because low average error does not guarantee the same reliability on every line or in every period; calibration and width assessment provide the framework \citep{gneitingcal2007,gneiting2007,angelopoulos2021,barber2021,barber2023}, and subgroup evaluation is essential when decisions are taken line by line.

Previous work has thus addressed the physical drivers of pelleting energy demand, energy performance indicators, production-adjusted baselines, machine-learning prediction, temporal forecasting, explainability and uncertainty quantification, but largely in isolation. It remains unclear whether an explainable production-adjusted baseline beats line-specific SEC for heterogeneous feed-pelleting operations under strict chronological validation, whether completed same-line histories add predictive value beyond current-operation variables, and whether regression, nonlinear, temporal and pretrained models can be compared on the same eligible records while interpretability and uncertainty are assessed together.

This study develops and chronologically evaluates explainable, production-adjusted electricity baselines from real operational records of three industrial feed-pelleting lines. Simple operational references, regularized regression, nonlinear ensembles, locally trained temporal models and pretrained time-series configurations are evaluated in one framework. The validated scope is retrospective assessment at the closure of recorded operations, when consolidated production descriptors and outcomes are available; prospective monitoring is a later application that requires confirming predictor availability at the intended origin and validating the full procedure on a new consecutive period. The main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item production-adjusted baselines for heterogeneous operation-level feed-pelleting records, compared with line-specific SEC references;
\item an assessment of whether completed same-line operating histories add predictive information beyond current-operation variables;
\item chronological development and testing that expose model stability across later periods and individual pelleting lines;
\item global and line-specific residual intervals that quantify predictive uncertainty; and
\item record-level interpretability through an exact additive decomposition of the Ridge predictor.
\end{enumerate}
For a plant, the framework separates the ordinary variability of electricity use that follows from production conditions from deviations that deserve a closer look. It supports energy-performance monitoring, identification of atypical operations, comparison among pelleting lines and prioritization of operational reviews. Because the baseline is production-adjusted, chronologically evaluated and accompanied by uncertainty and record-level interpretability, it can also serve as a statistical component of energy-management platforms or industrial digital twins once prospective input availability and measurement boundaries have been verified.
