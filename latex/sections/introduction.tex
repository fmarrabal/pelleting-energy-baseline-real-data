\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Two pelleting operations run on the same day can use very different amounts of electricity for entirely ordinary reasons: one processes more material, runs a different product, or takes place on another line. Before a plant can say anything about efficiency, those differences have to be accounted for. In feed manufacturing the usual tool is a specific energy consumption (SEC) in kWh/t. It is simple and easy to communicate, but it cannot separate the effects of product mix, line assignment, processed mass and batch structure, so an operation that looks wasteful may simply have been an unusual one. Energy management standards are explicit about the alternative: ISO 50001 asks for energy performance to be judged against a baseline normalized for the relevant variables, and ISO 50006 and the IPMVP describe how such baselines and performance indicators are built and used \citep{iso50001,iso50006,ipmvp2022}. The stakes are not small. Food processing in the United States consumes about 75~TWh of electricity a year, and electricity accounts for roughly 30\% of the reported energy use of UK food and beverage manufacturing \citep{compton2018,foster2023,park2021}.

Pelleting is a good place to build such a baseline because its electricity demand depends so strongly on what is being processed. Pellet quality, conditioning, feed composition, steam application, temperature, particle characteristics and operating conditions all shape pelleting behaviour and mechanical energy demand \citep{thomas1996,thomas1997,thomas2020,skoch1981,bastiaansen2023}. Those studies, however, are about material properties, process behaviour or pellet quality. None of them builds an operation-level electricity baseline from real plant records and tests it, in time order, against the reference a plant actually uses. That is the gap addressed here.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}
Regression-based baselines for measurement and verification go back at least to the monthly-data models of Kissock and Eger \citep{kissock2008}. Automated baselines on interval data later showed that predictive accuracy and honest uncertainty decide whether a saving can be claimed at all \citep{granderson2016,gallagher2018}, and key-performance-indicator frameworks carry the same idea into production facilities \citep{cosgrove2018}. In the feed industry itself, S\'anchez et al.\ \citep{sanchez2020} built motor-level efficiency assessments, energy baselines and performance indicators for a balanced-feed plant (about 10~kWh/t at 500~t/day, with an estimated saving potential of 364,000~kWh/year), but at an aggregated process level rather than per operation. Wigger et al.\ \citep{wigger2025} showed how AI models can be integrated into the energy management system of a manufacturing company from its existing data, with consumption forecasting and anomaly detection as the use cases; their object is the company's consumption series, not the individual operation and what it processed.

On the modelling side, regularized regression gives stable and interpretable estimates when predictors are correlated \citep{hoerl1970}, while tree ensembles and neural networks capture nonlinearities and interactions \citep{friedman2001}. The study closest to ours is that of Ekwaro-Osire et al.\ \citep{ekwaro2025}, who compared linear regression, tree-based methods and a multilayer perceptron on about 1,100 production batches with 67 input characteristics from an industrial compound-feed pelleting process and found that the more complex models did not necessarily reduce error. Because it relied on shuffled cross-validation, it could not say how the models behave on a later period, and it did not consider operating histories, pretrained temporal models, predictive uncertainty or record-level traceability. Panjapornpon et al.\ \citep{panjapornpon2023} predicted the energy efficiency of a petrochemical process with an explainable transfer-learning LSTM made robust to sensor faults, but on regularly sampled signals rather than on heterogeneous completed operations. In this journal, production energy under complex working conditions has been predicted with an attention-based recurrent network \citep{yang2024eaai}, the consumption of hot-die-forging lines with a hybrid deep architecture \citep{wang2026forging}, and energy-prediction models have been deployed in manufacturing through asset administration shells \citep{shin2024eaai}. Our setting differs in two respects: the inputs are the consolidated production descriptors that a plant database already holds rather than operating signals, and the question is whether richer models add anything to a linear, exactly decomposable baseline when both receive the same information under a frozen chronological protocol.

The Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) \citep{lim2021} and N-HiTS \citep{challu2023} are the reference neural forecasters with covariates, and foundation models such as TimesFM and Chronos transfer representations learned from large time-series collections to new tasks \citep{das2024,ansari2024}. Recent benchmarks have begun to map where these models help. The FETS benchmark evaluates foundation models against task-specific learners across 54 energy datasets \citep{obermeier2026}; zero-shot Chronos and TimesFM variants have been compared with tuned LSTMs and conventional models for electricity prices \citep{miseta2026,lin2026}; and foundation models have been compared for monthly precipitation forecasting \citep{song2026tsfm}. Comparative frameworks also stress the evaluation design itself, whether fixed-origin, expanding or rolling windows against linear and naive baselines \citep{micallef2025}, the effects of context length and horizon \citep{kavoosighafi2026}, or transfer and zero-shot strategies \citep{neubauer2026}. All of these operate on regularly sampled series. Industrial pelleting records are different. Consecutive completed operations have different masses, products, lines, start times and durations, so the prediction origin and the information available at that moment have to be fixed before any model can be compared \citep{hewamalage2023}, and random or shuffled partitions inflate apparent performance \citep{tashman2000,cerqueira2020,bergmeir2018}.

Explainable AI for energy systems has a growing literature \citep{machlev2022,saarela2024,cacao2025}, from frameworks that validate what data-driven power-curve models have learned \citep{letzgus2024} to permutation feature importance combined with conformal prediction to make explanations robust in predictive process monitoring \citep{mehdiyev2025}. Prediction intervals complete the picture. Conformal methods have been compared for electricity prices and solar power \citep{oconnor2025,nam2025} and used to calibrate multi-energy load forecasts \citep{huang2026conformal}, but their guarantees weaken under temporal dependence and drift \citep{angelopoulos2021,barber2023,barber2021}, so coverage has to be read together with width and checked by subgroup \citep{gneitingcal2007,gneiting2007}. Digital twins are the natural destination of a validated baseline \citep{tao2019}, provided its statistical component can be trusted.

As far as we know, no previous work combines real operation-level records, chronological validation with a frozen test, an exactly decomposable baseline, residual-calibrated intervals checked by line, and a matched comparison with temporal and foundation models.

\subsection{Objective and contributions}\label{sec:contrib}
Our objective is to establish, on real operational records of three industrial feed-pelleting lines, whether an explainable, production-adjusted electricity baseline predicts the energy of a completed operation better than the line-specific SEC reference used in practice, and whether histories of completed operations, modern temporal models or pretrained foundation models add anything to it. The paper makes three contributions. The first is a production-adjusted baseline for heterogeneous operations validated with a frozen chronological test: seven estimators are compared on 800 chronological development targets, the selected Ridge regression is frozen together with its interval calibration, and it is then evaluated once on 798 later records against line-specific SEC. Previous feed-industry baselines are aggregated \citep{sanchez2020} or validated by shuffling \citep{ekwaro2025}. The second is a matched test of operating histories, temporal models and foundation models on irregular industrial records: TFT, N-HiTS, TimesFM~2.5 and Chronos-2 receive exactly the same 16 completed same-line operations and the same current descriptors as the linear control, on 584 identical targets, with the information available at the origin fixed in advance. Existing foundation-model benchmarks concern regularly sampled series \citep{obermeier2026,miseta2026,lin2026}. The third is record-level explainability and calibrated uncertainty that survive the chronological test: every estimate decomposes exactly into production, product, line and calendar terms, and residual-calibrated 90\% and 95\% intervals are assessed by line and by week, which is what allows an unusual operation to be told apart from an unusual product mix.

The validated scope is retrospective assessment at the closure of recorded operations. Prospective monitoring is a later application and would first require confirming which predictors are available at the intended origin. For a plant, the result is a baseline that separates the ordinary variability of electricity use from deviations worth a closer look, and that can serve as the statistical component of an energy-management platform or digital twin.
