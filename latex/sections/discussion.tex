\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Production adjustment against intensity-based references}
During development, Ridge produced lower prediction errors than the line-specific median, recent SEC, MLP, ExtraTrees and line-specific SEC references and performed like histogram gradient boosting; in the frozen chronological test its MAE was 14.42\% below line-specific SEC. This agrees with energy-baseline research that recommends adjusting consumption for the relevant operating conditions rather than relying on aggregate or intensity-based indicators \citep{kissock2008,granderson2016,gallagher2018,cosgrove2018}, and it extends the pelleting studies that link energy demand to production volume, formulation, conditioning, equipment and operating conditions \citep{skoch1981,thomas1996,thomas1997,thomas2020,bastiaansen2023}. The contribution is a chronologically evaluated baseline that combines production mass, product characteristics and line identity for heterogeneous completed operations, which is the kind of normalization that ISO 50006 and the IPMVP expect of an energy baseline \citep{iso50006,ipmvp2022}. The improvement is in prediction accuracy: it is neither a reduction in actual energy use nor an estimate of achievable savings, and Ridge was chosen for its balance of accuracy, parsimony and traceability rather than for universal superiority over nonlinear models.

\subsection{Line-specific behaviour}
Production adjustment helped on all three lines. Pellet~2 and Pellet~3 showed the most consistent agreement between predictions and observations and are the strongest basis for prospective validation. Pellet~1 also improved on line-specific SEC, but its remaining negative bias shows that the frozen model was not operationally calibrated for that line. This localized requirement does not undermine the common modelling framework; it shows that aggregate performance can hide line-specific deficiencies. Differences among lines may reflect equipment configuration, production allocation, maintenance, metering or unrecorded operating conditions and should not be read as intrinsic differences in energy efficiency.

\subsection{Operating histories, temporal models and foundation models}
Under the tested 16-record, same-line, within-week representation, completed-operation histories did not improve on current-record covariates. Dosed mass, product characteristics and line identity may already capture much of the repeatable structure, and irregular intervals weaken the meaning of sequential proximity. The conclusion is confined to the evaluated short-context representation and does not contradict the value of temporal models where sequences carry informative dependence \citep{lim2021,challu2023}; likewise, the TimesFM and Chronos results characterize the tested interfaces, not the general capabilities of foundation models \citep{das2024,ansari2024,ansari2025}. The XReg extrapolation and the device-dependent normalization sensitivity of Chronos-2 (Appendix~\ref{sec:computing}) show that covariate adaptation and normalization shape the final industrial prediction as much as the pretrained weights do, and that a model's ability to accept the available covariates is no evidence that its pipeline is stable for this kind of record. Longer contexts, explicit elapsed time, operating-state information, maintenance events and regularly sampled measurements would allow a fairer assessment of temporal information in industrial pelleting \citep{aksu2024,zeng2023}.

\subsection{Reading the additive decomposition}
Ridge gives an exact additive decomposition of its fitted linear predictor and therefore a direct, reproducible audit trail. Its coefficients and record-specific contributions nevertheless depend on scaling, categorical coding, reference categories, feature construction and correlations among predictors \citep{rudin2019,aas2021,fisher2019}: they describe the fitted prediction function, do not identify causal effects and would change under alternative coding choices. Line and product terms may also absorb unrecorded differences in formulation, equipment condition, production allocation or operating practice. The small exploratory gains from product-dependent mass effects and residual correction indicate where the next refinement may come from, but they are hypotheses for a new period, not reasons to replace the frozen model on the strength of an already examined test.

\subsection{Uncertainty and the meaning of a flagged record}
The residual intervals showed that satisfactory marginal coverage does not guarantee comparable line-specific calibration \citep{barber2021,angelopoulos2021,barber2023}. Pellet~2 had the most stable coverage, whereas Pellet~1 and, to a lesser extent, Pellet~3 need further assessment. Rolling calibration raised coverage mainly by widening the intervals, which confirms that coverage must be read together with interval width, bias and alert frequency \citep{gneitingcal2007,gneiting2007}. An interval exceedance is therefore a candidate for technical review, not a confirmed anomaly, inefficiency, equipment failure or energy-saving opportunity.

\subsection{Scope of use and digital-twin integration}
The evidence supports retrospective assessment after each recorded operation closes: an expected electricity value, an empirically calibrated residual interval and a traceable deviation that identifies candidates for review. Prospective supervisory alerts require validation over a new consecutive production period with prespecified line-level criteria and, for Pellet~1, additional recalibration or predictors. Automatic control and modification of process set-points remain outside the evidence, because the observational model does not estimate causal responses. A digital-twin application should therefore begin with validated monitoring and decision support \citep{tao2019}, storing the input and model version of each issued estimate and attaching the measured outcome when it becomes available, so that an earlier forecast and a later production-adjusted assessment remain distinguishable.

\subsection{Strengths and limitations}
The study draws its strength from real industrial records, chronological separation, operational comparators, line-specific diagnostics, residual intervals and a reproducible decomposition. Its transferability is limited by the absence of a verified production-order identifier, retained temporal overlaps, unverified meter boundaries and undocumented predictor-availability times. The data cover three lines of one plant over about three months, use product categories rather than complete formulations, and lack detailed maintenance and operating-state variables. The intervals mainly assess marginal coverage, several extensions were post hoc, and reuse of the operational records beyond reproduction of this study depends on the data owner (see Data availability). The contribution therefore lies in the evaluation framework---production adjustment, chronological testing, line-specific assessment, uncertainty quantification and algebraic traceability---rather than in a universally deployable fitted model.
