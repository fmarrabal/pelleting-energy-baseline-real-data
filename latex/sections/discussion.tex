\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Production adjustment and what it changed on each line}
During development, Ridge produced lower prediction errors than the line-specific median, the recent-SEC estimator, the MLP, ExtraTrees and line-specific SEC, and performed like histogram gradient boosting; in the frozen chronological test its MAE was 14.42\% below line-specific SEC. This is in line with the energy-baseline literature, which has long recommended adjusting consumption for the relevant operating conditions instead of relying on aggregate or intensity-based indicators \citep{kissock2008,granderson2016,gallagher2018,cosgrove2018}, and it extends the pelleting studies that link energy demand to production volume, formulation, conditioning, equipment and operating conditions \citep{skoch1981,thomas1996,thomas1997,thomas2020,bastiaansen2023}. What is added is a chronologically evaluated baseline that combines production mass, product characteristics and line identity for heterogeneous completed operations, which is the normalization that ISO 50006 and the IPMVP expect of an energy baseline \citep{iso50006,ipmvp2022}. Two qualifications should be kept in view. The improvement is in prediction accuracy, not in actual energy use, and it is not an estimate of achievable savings. And Ridge was chosen for its balance of accuracy, parsimony and traceability, not because it was shown to beat nonlinear models in general.

Production adjustment helped on all three lines, but not equally. Pellet~2 and Pellet~3 showed the most consistent agreement between predictions and observations and are the strongest basis for a prospective validation. Pellet~1 also improved on line-specific SEC, yet its remaining negative bias means the frozen model was not operationally calibrated for that line during the test period. That is a local requirement, not a failure of the common framework, and it illustrates how an aggregate score can hide a line-specific deficiency. Differences among lines may reflect equipment configuration, production allocation, maintenance, metering or unrecorded operating conditions, and should not be read as differences in intrinsic energy efficiency.

\subsection{Operating histories, temporal models and foundation models}
Under the tested representation (16 completed same-line operations within the validation week), histories did not improve on current-record covariates. One plausible reason is that dosed mass, product characteristics and line identity already capture most of the repeatable structure; another is that irregular intervals between operations weaken whatever meaning sequential proximity might have. The conclusion is confined to the evaluated short-context representation and does not contradict the value of temporal models where sequences carry informative dependence \citep{lim2021,challu2023}; likewise, the TimesFM and Chronos results characterize the tested interfaces, not the general capabilities of foundation models \citep{das2024,ansari2024,ansari2025}. The XReg extrapolation and the device-dependent normalization sensitivity of Chronos-2 (Appendix~\ref{sec:computing}) do carry a more general lesson: covariate adaptation and normalization shape the final industrial prediction as much as the pretrained weights do, and the fact that a model accepts the available covariates says nothing about whether its pipeline is stable for this kind of record. A fairer assessment of temporal information in pelleting would need longer contexts, explicit elapsed time, operating-state information, maintenance events and regularly sampled measurements \citep{aksu2024,zeng2023}.

\subsection{Reading the decomposition and the intervals}
Ridge gives an exact additive decomposition of its fitted linear predictor, so each estimate can be traced back to its inputs directly and reproducibly. Its coefficients and record-specific contributions nevertheless depend on scaling, categorical coding, reference categories, feature construction and correlations among predictors \citep{rudin2019,aas2021,fisher2019}: they describe the fitted prediction function, do not identify causal effects, and would change under alternative coding choices. Line and product terms may also absorb unrecorded differences in formulation, equipment condition, production allocation or operating practice. The small exploratory gains from product-dependent mass effects and residual correction point to where the next refinement may come from, but they are hypotheses for a new period rather than reasons to replace the frozen model on the strength of a test that has already been examined.

The residual intervals made a related point. Satisfactory marginal coverage did not guarantee comparable calibration on each line \citep{barber2021,angelopoulos2021,barber2023}: Pellet~2 had the most stable coverage, whereas Pellet~1 and, to a lesser extent, Pellet~3 need further assessment. Rolling calibration raised coverage mainly by widening the intervals, which is a reminder that coverage has to be read together with interval width, bias and alert frequency \citep{gneitingcal2007,gneiting2007}. An interval exceedance is therefore a candidate for technical review. It is not, by itself, a confirmed anomaly, an inefficiency, an equipment failure or an energy-saving opportunity.

\subsection{Scope of use, limitations and digital-twin integration}
The evidence supports retrospective assessment after each recorded operation closes: an expected electricity value, an empirically calibrated residual interval and a traceable deviation that identifies candidates for review. Prospective supervisory alerts would require validation over a new consecutive production period with prespecified line-level criteria and, for Pellet~1, additional recalibration or predictors. Automatic control and modification of process set-points remain outside the evidence, because an observational model does not estimate causal responses. A digital-twin application should therefore begin with validated monitoring and decision support \citep{tao2019}, storing the inputs and model version of each issued estimate and attaching the measured outcome when it becomes available, so that an earlier forecast and a later production-adjusted assessment remain distinguishable.

The study draws its strength from real industrial records, chronological separation, operational comparators, line-specific diagnostics, residual intervals and a reproducible decomposition. Its transferability is limited by the absence of a verified production-order identifier, by retained temporal overlaps, by unverified meter boundaries and by undocumented predictor-availability times. The data cover three lines of one plant over about three months, use product categories rather than complete formulations, and lack detailed maintenance and operating-state variables. The intervals mainly assess marginal coverage, several extensions were post hoc, and the operational records cannot be released publicly, although they are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request. The contribution therefore lies in the evaluation framework, with its production adjustment, chronological testing, line-specific assessment, uncertainty quantification and algebraic traceability, rather than in a fitted model that could be deployed elsewhere as it stands.
